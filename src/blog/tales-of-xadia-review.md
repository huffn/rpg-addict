When our group wrapped up our Tales of Xadia campaign, I found myself in a funny spot. I loved the world, I loved our characters, and I had a great time with my friends, but when people ask if I'd recommend the system, the most honest answer I've got is "maybe." I want to try to explain why.

For this campaign, [The Corrupted Core](/campaigns/ToX/), I played Telemachus, an exiled noble turned mountain farmer who is a large gentle giant, and he works very hard to not hurt anyone. He also ended up being the party's cook, which is not a job anyone gave him so much as a job he refused to let anyone else have. Like my [City of Mist review](/blog/city-of-mist-review/), this is mostly a player's take, plus some of what I picked up from trying hard to understand the rules.

## You Don't Need to Have Watched the Show
Tales of Xadia is set in the world of The Dragon Prince, and I know that might make some people nervous if they haven't seen it. You'll be fine. The book is written from the point of view of the end of season three of the show, and it gives you pretty much everything you need to know about the world.

Honestly, this is where the book shines. They captured the feel of the show and turned it into a campaign book. The art is amazing and evocative, and the lore felt like it was giving us answers instead of leaving us with a pile of possible contradictions to sort out. With some licensed games, I feel like you spend a lot of time guessing how the world is supposed to work. This one just tells you.

## Getting Everyone to the Table
Getting started went a lot smoother than I expected. We got to use the digital character creation tools, so building our characters felt like a guided tour instead of a pile of choices with no idea where to start. If you're new to Cortex, I'd definitely recommend using them.

The book itself is also laid out much better than some systems we've played (I'm looking at you, City of Mist). I was still glad we had the PDF, mostly because I could remember the rule I wanted but never which page it was on.

One thing the included adventure doesn't really do is explain why the party is together. We used a prequel adventure that was provided online as our session zero, and then we mostly hand-waved how our group formed. Our party was a mix of humans and elves, which at this point in the world's timeline is something that has only just started happening, so we decided not to think too hard about it and let the story sort it out.

## Describe It First, Find the Dice Second
Tales of Xadia runs on the Cortex system, and the part of it I liked best is that it's a heavily narrative game where you describe what you want to do and then figure out which dice apply. You aren't scanning a list of abilities trying to find one that kind of fits. You decide what your character is doing, and then you build a dice pool out of your traits that matches it.

**Resource dice** fit into that really well too. A healing potion might start out as a d10, and after you use it, it steps down to a d8. You can feel the resource running low without having to keep track of a number on your sheet. It's a small thing, but those were the parts of the system that felt the smoothest for us.

## The Time the Dice Said No (in a Good Way)
Near the end of our campaign, there was a moment where everything was coming to a head, and I decided Telemachus was willing to sacrifice himself to solve the problem the whole story had been building toward. I'll keep the details vague since it's part of the published adventure, but I was fully committed. The gentle giant was going to go out a hero.

The dice decided he should survive.

That could have felt like an anticlimax, but it didn't. It felt earned. The important part was that he was willing to give everything, and the system let that choice matter without forcing one particular outcome. Telemachus got to be a hero and also go back to cooking for everyone, which I think is the ending he would have picked anyway.

## Where It Gets Fiddly
Before the game ever hit the table, I was pretty excited about the rules. Reading through the book, everything sounded really elegant. Stress that tracks how your character is feeling, challenges that can be anything from a dragon to a crumbling tower, goals that grow your character instead of experience points. On paper, it all seemed to click together. It was only once we were actually using it, session after session, that the seams started to show.

Here's where my "maybe" comes from. Cortex felt to me like a bunch of smaller subsystems that almost fit together to make a really good system. Building dice pools and using resource dice worked well for us. **Challenges** and **stress**, on the other hand, felt more like separate minigames attached to the side.

Stress was the big one for us. There are six different kinds to keep track of: afraid, angry, anxious, corrupted, exhausted, and injured. Each one is its own pool, and making sure we were tracking all of them correctly was fiddly enough that it pulled us out of the story more often than it added to it. At some point it starts to feel less like an emotional journey and more like filling out a form about your emotional journey.

**Goals** were the other sticking point. As players we would come up with our own goals, and that part was great. But deciding how difficult each goal was, to figure out how much effort it would take to finish, sometimes felt like splitting hairs.

**Dark Magic** was definitely the most complicated part of the system at our table, and it's also where a lot of that corrupted stress came from. Our dark magic user was a very experienced roleplayer, so it never felt overwhelming for us, but I feel like it could be a lot for someone newer to the hobby. If someone in your group wants to play a dark mage, it's worth making sure they're comfortable with crunchier rules, or that the GM is ready to help them through it.

None of this is broken. It just sometimes felt complicated for the sake of being complicated, and there were times when the mechanics were getting in the way of the thing we actually showed up for, which was telling a good story with our friends.

## What Would Turn My Maybe Into a Yes
I feel like the fix isn't that big. If you simplify stress down to fewer types and don't worry too much about exactly how hard each goal is, a lot of the friction goes away, and what's left is a gorgeous world with a narrative core that works. If you love the setting and have a GM who's willing to streamline things, I think Tales of Xadia gets pretty close to a yes.

## Play It If…
- You love The Dragon Prince, or you just want a fantasy world with great art and lore that gives you answers
- You like describing what your character does first and figuring out the mechanics second
- Your group is comfortable house-ruling things to keep them simple
- You can use the digital character creation tools to get everyone started

## Skip It If…
- You want a light system that mostly stays out of your way
- Tracking six kinds of stress sounds more like homework than fun
- You'd rather have one tight system than several subsystems bolted together
