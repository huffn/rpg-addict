When our group wrapped up our Tales of Xadia campaign, I found myself in a funny spot. I loved the world, I loved our characters, and I had a great time with my friends, but when people ask if I'd recommend the system, the most honest answer I've got is "maybe."

For this campaign, [The Corrupted Core](/campaigns/ToX/), I played Telemachus, an exiled noble turned mountain farmer who is a large gentle giant, and he works very hard to not hurt anyone. He also ended up being the party's cook. Like my [City of Mist review](/blog/city-of-mist-review/), this is mostly a player's take, plus some of what I picked up from trying hard to understand the rules.

## You Don't Need to Have Watched the Show
Tales of Xadia is set in the world of The Dragon Prince, which might make some people nervous if they haven't seen it. The book is written from the point of view of the end of season three of the show, and it gives you pretty much everything you need to know about the world.

This is where the book shines. They captured the feel of the show and turned it into a campaign book. The art is amazing and evocative, and the lore felt like it was giving us answers instead of leaving us with a pile of possible contradictions to sort out. With some licensed games, you spend a lot of time guessing how the world is supposed to work.

## Getting Everyone to the Table
Getting started went smoother than I expected. We got to use the digital character creation tools, so building our characters felt like a guided tour instead of a pile of choices with no idea where to start. If you're new to Cortex, I'd recommend using them.

The book is also laid out much better than some systems we've played (I'm looking at you, City of Mist). I was still glad we had the PDF, mostly because I could remember the rule I wanted but never which page it was on.

The included adventure doesn't really explain why the party is together. We used a prequel adventure that was provided online as our session zero, and then mostly hand-waved how our group formed. Our party was a mix of humans and elves, which at this point in the world's timeline has only just started happening, so we let the story sort it out.

## Describe It First, Find the Dice Second
Tales of Xadia runs on the Cortex system, and the part I liked best is that it's a heavily narrative game where you describe what you want to do and then figure out which dice apply. You decide what your character is doing, and then you build a dice pool out of your traits that matches it.

**Resource dice** fit into that well too. A healing potion might start out as a d10, and after you use it, it steps down to a d8, so you can feel the resource running low without keeping track of a number on your sheet.

## The Time the Dice Said No (in a Good Way)
Near the end of our campaign, everything was coming to a head, and I decided Telemachus was willing to sacrifice himself to solve the problem the whole story had been building toward. I'll keep the details vague since it's part of the published adventure, but I was fully committed, and the dice decided he should survive.

It felt earned. The important part was that he was willing to give everything, and the system let that choice matter without forcing one particular outcome. Telemachus got to be a hero and go back to cooking for everyone.

## Where It Gets Fiddly
Before the game ever hit the table, I was pretty excited about the rules. Reading through the book, everything sounded elegant: stress that tracks how your character is feeling, challenges that can be anything from a dragon to a crumbling tower, goals that grow your character instead of experience points. It was only once we were using it, session after session, that the seams started to show.

Cortex felt to me like a bunch of smaller subsystems that almost fit together to make a really good system. Building dice pools and using resource dice worked well for us. **Challenges** and **stress** felt more like separate minigames attached to the side.

Stress was the big one for us. There are six different kinds to keep track of: afraid, angry, anxious, corrupted, exhausted, and injured. Each one is its own pool, and tracking all of them correctly was fiddly enough that it pulled us out of the story more often than it added to it.

**Goals** were the other sticking point. As players we would come up with our own goals, and that part was great, but deciding how difficult each goal was, to figure out how much effort it would take to finish, sometimes felt like splitting hairs.

**Dark Magic** was the most complicated part of the system at our table, and it's also where a lot of that corrupted stress came from. Our dark magic user was a very experienced roleplayer, so it never felt overwhelming for us, but I feel like it could be a lot for someone newer to the hobby. If someone in your group wants to play a dark mage, make sure they're comfortable with crunchier rules, or that the GM is ready to help them through it.

Sometimes it felt complicated for the sake of being complicated, and there were times when the mechanics got in the way of what we actually showed up for, which was telling a good story with our friends.

## From Behind the Screen
I asked our GM what she thought of running it, and she had a lot more good to say about it than I expected.

She liked that there was usually more than one way to solve a problem, and that it didn't always come down to a fight. The published adventure helped with that by giving her ideas for what the party might do, even when we went a completely different direction. It also had easter eggs tied to what was happening in the show, without limiting what we could do as players.

She also liked how the dice pools come together. You pick an attribute, a value, and a distinction, add two of the dice together for your total, and the third becomes the effect die that says how strong the result is. Because so much of that comes from who your character is, your backstory plays a bigger role than in most games, since you're using it in your rolls.

Her complaints lined up with mine. She thought the different types of damage could be simplified, and she felt leveling up wasn't structured enough. You work toward your goals during a story, but you have to wait until the end of that story before you can spend what you earned, so growth can feel a long way off.

If you simplify stress down to fewer types and don't worry too much about exactly how hard each goal is, a lot of the friction goes away, and what's left is a gorgeous world with a narrative core that works. If you love the setting and have a GM who's willing to streamline things, I think Tales of Xadia gets pretty close to a yes.

## Play It If…
- You love The Dragon Prince, or you want a fantasy world with great art and lore that gives you answers
- You like describing what your character does first and figuring out the mechanics second
- You want your backstory to matter in your rolls, and problems that don't always end in a fight
- Your group is comfortable house-ruling things to keep them simple
- You can use the digital character creation tools to get everyone started

## Skip It If…
- You want a light system that mostly stays out of your way
- You like leveling up often, not only at the end of a story
- Tracking six kinds of stress sounds more like homework than fun
- You'd rather have one tight system than several subsystems bolted together
