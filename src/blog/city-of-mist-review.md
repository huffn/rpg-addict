When our group finished our City of Mist campaign, I realized I had a lot of feelings about the system, both good and bad. We came in as D&D players and spent the first few months trying to figure out what we were doing, so I want to share what worked for us, what didn't, and whether I'd recommend it. (Spoiler: I would, with a few asterisks.)

For about a year and a half I played Mira Eros in our campaign, [Sons of Catalina](/campaigns/CoM/). She was a hematology doctor whose rift was Dracula, which meant she spent her days at the hospital around a lot of blood and was very professional about it. I wasn't the MC, so this is mostly a player's take, but I did a lot of digging into the rules along the way, so I'll share some of what I learned about the system as a whole too.

## Characters That Grow Up (and Sometimes Die)
The thing I keep coming back to is how characters change over the course of a campaign. In most games I've played, your character's growth lives in your backstory and in your head, and the sheet just gets bigger numbers. In City of Mist, every character is split between their **Mythos**, the legend living inside them, and their **Logos**, their everyday life. As the story pushes on those themes, some of them fade and get replaced with new ones, so the sheet ends up telling the story of who your character is turning into.

For Mira, that happened in a way I definitely didn't plan. She started out with a **Blood Doctor** Logos theme, and most of her tags were about healing people. Then she [died](/campaigns/CoM/Mira/). Her Dracula rift brought her back, which was great, but it turns out the hospital isn't super flexible about employees who were recently dead. She lost her Blood Doctor theme and picked up a new Mythos theme, **Not On My Watch**, with tags about fighting back against the villains instead of patching people up afterwards.

I feel like that's the best thing the system does. Something big happened in the story, and the character sheet changed to match it, and nobody had to sit down and negotiate it. The mechanics were just already pointed in that direction.

I do like to do things a little extra, so I made my own character sheets for Mira, printed them out, and laminated them for the table. Here's the before and after. Click either one to see it full size.

If you compare the two, you can see her slide from Mythos 2 and Logos 2 to Mythos 3 and Logos 1. Blood Doctor, with tags like *Medical doctor*, *Keep cool in an emergency*, and *Treat injuries*, is gone. In its place is Not On My Watch, with *A superior combatant*, *Protecting the weak*, and *Defensive stance*, plus a new mystery for Mira to chew on: "What could justify my violence?" Even the little tag combo cheat sheet I made for myself changed, from *Heal Someone* to *Protect the Weak*. She was still Mira, just a lot less interested in bedside manner.

## Stop. Holding. Back.
The reason Mira died is also one of my favorite mechanics. When a character wants to do something drastic, beyond what the normal moves cover, they have to **Stop Holding Back**, and it costs something real. Mira used it in the crypts to save two people she cared about, and it felt appropriately powerful while also having consequences. The consequence in this case was a sword to the face.

For a few sessions after that, I played Detective Flynn Perry, a hardboiled detective whose rift was Perry the Platypus. Flynn joined the crew just in time to help investigate Mira's death, which led to them discovering that Mira wasn't quite as dead as everyone thought. Flynn's career with the 86ers was short, but I like to think he made an impact.

Of course Flynn got a laminated sheet too. His Mythos themes were *As Quiet as an Aquatic Mammal* and *Let Me Check My Utility Belt*, his Logos themes were *Never Without My Fedora* and *Knight in Sour Armor*, and his weakness tags included *Mounting paperwork*, which I feel like is the most realistic thing about any detective I've played.

## The Parts of Every Session I Looked Forward To
Two small things made almost every session feel special.

The first was the **monologue**. Each player got to open the session with a short monologue moment for their character. It doesn't sound like much, but it meant every player got the spotlight at least once a session, and the little threads we started in those monologues kept getting picked up later.

Our table added one extra rule: every monologue came with a song. I got to do three monologues for Mira, and honestly I spent more time outside of sessions picking those songs than I spent on almost anything else for the campaign. I wanted each one to fit the story I was trying to tell.

- **"Holding Out for a Hero" (the Aviators cover)** kicked off the very first player monologue of the campaign. It introduced Mira and how being a vampire was getting in the way of her daily life as a doctor.
- **"Poison" by Alice Cooper** went with the monologue where I added Mira's backstory: her marriage to Solomon Grunderson, who tragically died of radiation poisoning while working for a corporation. That corporation turned out to be part of the big bad of the campaign, which I did not see coming when I picked the song.
- **"ONE MORE TIME" by blink-182** was for a monologue where Mira tried to come to terms with whether the golem we had recently fought had been her husband, while also having to keep raiding the hospital's blood bank to keep her vampire urges under control. A normal week for Mira.

I feel like the songs did a lot of the heavy lifting. Even when the monologue itself was short, the song set the mood for the whole session, and everyone at the table looked forward to hearing what each person had picked.

The second was **Geeking Out**. At the end of each session, the players share what they loved about the session and about each other's characters. I already do something like this in every campaign I run, which I call *Stars and Wishes*. Everyone shares what they really liked about the other players that session, and the hope is that the good stuff keeps happening next time. Finding it built right into a rulebook made me pretty happy.

The **crew theme**, which tracks how the group grows together, didn't land the same way for us. Ours ended up as a tally at the end of the session, and it felt more like bookkeeping than anything. If your group wants to keep things lighter, I feel like that's the part you could trim without losing much.

## Unlearning D&D
The hardest part of City of Mist for our group wasn't any one rule. It was unlearning our D&D habits.

In D&D, if you miss an attack you just swing again next turn. In City of Mist, you can't repeat an action using the same tags unless the situation has changed. That sounds simple, but it changes how you think about every roll. Every action needs to leave a path forward whether you succeed or fail. "I attack the monster" isn't really enough on its own. You have to figure out how your action is changing the scene, so that if you do need to attack the monster again, it's because the story has moved forward, and not because you're hoping the dice like you better this time.

Combat lives in a weird middle space. It isn't tactical the way D&D or Pathfinder is, with a grid and hit points and positioning, but it's more structured than just narrating what happens. It took us a while to get comfortable there. (I wrote about some of our early struggles in [Investigating Core Moves in City of Mist](/blog/core-moves/).)

Right behind that was figuring out **tags and statuses**: which tags apply to what you're doing, how statuses build up, and how all of it changes the dice. We got there eventually, but it took a few sessions of figuring it out together.

## Some Things to Know Going In
**The MC does a lot of legwork.** Our MC used the Iceberg method the book lays out for building mysteries, and he liked the system. But I don't think he'd want to run another full campaign of it. He'd rather take the things that make City of Mist unique and bring them into other systems. I feel like that says something about both the prep load and how good the ideas are.

**The rulebook layout.** It isn't the worst rulebook we've tried to muddle through, but the things you need to look up are often in very different chapters when they probably should've been next to each other. By the end we had twenty-plus sticky notes poking out of our hard copies, and we still mostly searched the PDFs, because letting the computer search the whole document was way faster than flipping around the book. The sticky notes were more of a decorative choice at that point.

**It takes a while to click.** If your group is used to a fight every session with a clear tactical puzzle, the first few sessions are going to feel a little strange.

## Where I Landed
I'm really glad I played a campaign of City of Mist. Some of its ideas, like characters whose sheets change with the story, the opening monologues, and Geeking Out, are things I'm going to keep thinking about in every game I play or run. I'm also looking forward to seeing what the designers come out with next, and I'm hoping whatever it is helps simplify the rulebook a bit, so the next group doesn't need quite as many sticky notes.

## Play It If…
- You want characters whose growth shows up on the sheet, not just in the backstory
- Your group likes mysteries, investigation, and slow-burning reveals
- You want to try a narrative system that still has some structure to it
- Your MC enjoys improvising and building a mystery from the ground up

## Skip It If…
- You want tactical combat with a grid and hit points
- Your MC wants a game they can run with little prep
- Your group gets frustrated hunting through a rulebook, or you can't get the PDF
